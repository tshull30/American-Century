# American Century

A Victoria 3–style grand strategy game about running a company in the modern United States.

You play as a corporation: a fictional take on an Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla or Palantir, or a startup you found yourself. You hire workers from census-based **pops** ("Indian American software developers from Texas", "White engineers from Missouri"), build plants across all 50 states, sell into markets against rivals, research better products, climb the market-cap tiers and buy out the competition.

Deep and spreadsheet-y, but fast: daily ticks, five game speeds, and everything runs in the browser.

**New in v0.3:** about 160 cities with their own tax breaks, permit speed, wages, unions, power grids and data-center rules. A headquarters you shape (downtown tower, suburban campus or city block) with amenities. Locals who like or dislike each company. Pops that grow, migrate and retrain. A real election cycle on the 435 real House districts. Acquisitions that need board approval and can draw an antitrust second request.

**Since v0.3:** new candidates are named from the Football Coach / College Manager name pools (plus hand-typed women's names), matched to the background mix of their state. Quality-of-life additions: the game pauses on the events you pick (elections, laws, your deals, earnings, tier changes, recessions), a clickable list of things that need attention under the tabs, a Settings panel with autosave frequency, more keyboard shortcuts, event-log filters and search, a Best city button, and build choices that stick when you switch tabs.

**New in v0.4:** real production chains (oil and gas fields, refineries, steel mills, chemical plants, farms, solar panel factories, consumer goods) with state resources, 24 occupations instead of 9, industry know-how and economies of scale that make scattershot diversification expensive, local politics in every city (left, right and libertarian) that set policy and judge companies, and a pool of 4,300 real politicians and declared 2026 Senate contenders for candidates.

**New in v0.5:** the rest of the world. Twelve countries (China, Mexico, Canada, Vietnam, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Germany, Ireland, Saudi Arabia, Brazil) with their own pops and labor pools. Build plants abroad for cheaper labor, at lower productivity and with tariffs and freight on whatever you ship home; USMCA partners are duty-free until tariffs get heavy, China pays extra and is closed to chip, AI and research work. Several starting companies already make goods abroad. Country disruptions (a Taiwan Strait crisis, Chinese factory shutdowns, border slowdowns, a Gulf conflict), a World tab with US import sources, and immigrants who join American pops each year. Plus: automatic HQ growth, building filters and totals, quick level buttons, a confirmation before big builds, a next-earnings date, and a balance pass (an unknown seller can no longer charge premium prices).

**New in v0.6:** executives and a board of directors (hire, fire and poach a CEO, CFO, COO, CTO and CMO with skills and traits; a board that must approve big moves, loses faith when the stock lags, and faces activist investors and proxy fights at the annual meeting). A different cast every game: a random subset of the hand-written companies plus 45–60 generated ones across every industry, with a Shuffle button on the start screen. 32 new goods and 32 new buildings: coal, iron, copper, lithium, uranium and rare-earth mines, logging, ranches, cotton and dairy farms, aluminum smelters, sawmills, paper and textile mills, cement and fertilizer plants, auto parts, display fabs, meatpacking, wind turbines, homebuilders, furniture, appliances, beverages, restaurants, game studios, aircraft, defense, medical devices, cell networks, airlines and logistics, plus coal and wind power. `npx tsx scripts/economics.ts` prints every building's per-level economics.

**New in v0.7:** a newspaper and an annual report. Big news lands as a front page of *The American Ledger*: headline, deck and column stories, with your decisions printed right on the page. A stock ticker scrolls under the header. Each January brings an annual report with a letter to shareholders signed by your CEO, and every closed acquisition is filed as a stamped, signed Delaware certificate of merger. Company stories (recalls, data breaches, factory fires, union drives, strikes, lawsuits, spills, whistleblowers, ransomware, viral hits, FDA rulings, stadium deals, water protests) often ask you to choose, with follow-ups months later. 23 new world events (hurricanes, wildfires, droughts, polar vortexes, crashes and rallies, Fed shocks, housing booms and busts, bird flu, rare-earth bans, a Suez closure, a grid cyberattack and more). "Rest of state" is gone: every state is split into named regions with a county (the Permian Basin and West Texas, around Midland County), and every pop now says which county it lives in. Segments: restaurants are fast food, casual dining or fine dining, and apparel and cars have luxury lines, each bought by different incomes. Help text moved out of the panels into an encyclopedia (the ? on each panel, or <kbd>F1</kbd>). The Build panel was rebuilt: your own industries first, other industries one click away, the United States and abroad on separate tabs, and an order card with a stepper.

**New in v0.8:** the rest of the American economy. 61 new goods and services and 57 new buildings: banks, insurers, asset managers, payment networks and investment banks; hospitals, clinics, health insurers, senior living and diagnostics labs; big-box stores, supermarkets, e-commerce fulfillment, home centers, drugstores and gas stations; hotels (motel, full-service or resort), cruise lines, theme parks, casinos and sportsbooks, arenas, sports franchises, gyms and cinemas; cosmetics, salons, alcohol and tobacco; universities, prep schools and child care; apartments and office towers; water and waste utilities; cable and fiber, satellite internet, ride-hailing, railroads and rockets; heavy machinery, electrical equipment, robots, chipmaking tools, shipyards and tires; toys, sporting goods, pet food, jewelry, coffee shops, music and newsrooms; consulting, law, accounting, staffing, cybersecurity and commercial construction. Five new needs (banking and insurance, travel and leisure, personal care, alcohol and tobacco, education), five new research tracks and nine new industries. Finances are clearer: the header shows last month's profit, quarterly earnings compare revenue and profit with estimates analysts actually update (in line, beat or miss; a miss is not a loss), and headquarters and R&D show as costs instead of loss-making sites. Stores and restaurants can now restock from the wholesale market, small companies no longer carry an R&D lab they can't afford, and the monthly consumer pass is six times faster.

**New in v0.9:** people. Every executive, rival CEO and member of Congress has views on five issues (economy, labor, climate, trade, Big Tech) and an itemized opinion of your company. A new People tab shows dossiers: backgrounds and mottos for your (fictional) executives and rival CEOs, belief scales, and a ledger of exactly why each person likes or dislikes you. Members of Congress (real people, with stances modeled from their caucus faction) react to jobs and layoffs in their state, your lobbying, PAC money, offshoring, your energy footprint and Big Tech's power; friends lean your way on close votes, and hostile members call you to testify. Rival CEOs feud with you (and undercut your prices), take public swipes, offer two-year partnerships, and their boards ask a lower or higher takeover premium depending on how they feel. Your executives have morale: they ask for raises, perform better when happy, and quit when miserable. Also new: earnings guidance (lowball, realistic or bold) and 20 milestones with front-page headlines.

## Play

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # http://localhost:5173
```

`npm run build` produces one self-contained `dist/index.html` that you can host anywhere. Pushing to `main` deploys to GitHub Pages through `.github/workflows/deploy.yml`. Enable it once under **Settings → Pages → Source: GitHub Actions**.

Controls: <kbd>Space</kbd> pauses, <kbd>1</kbd>–<kbd>5</kbd> set the speed, <kbd>.</kbd> advances a day, <kbd>[</kbd> and <kbd>]</kbd> switch tabs, <kbd>?</kbd> opens Settings, <kbd>F1</kbd> opens the encyclopedia. The game autosaves to your browser every quarter by default; use **Export** to keep a save file.

## How it works

| System | What it does | Code |
|---|---|---|
| **Pops** | About 5,000 pops (state × culture × job) built by raking state demographics against state job mixes (iterative proportional fitting). Each pop has workers, dependents and a market wage. | `src/sim/pops.ts`, `src/sim/data/{states,cultures,jobs}.ts` |
| **City politics** | Each city has its residents' lean and a libertarian streak (hand-set; "rest of state" areas are derived from the 2024 state result). The council drifts with the national mood and sets tax breaks, permit speed, unions, wage floors, data-center deals (power abatements or surcharges, water rules, moratoriums) and, in the most progressive places, bans on new oil and gas. Residents judge companies by how they lobby and donate, whether they build fossil or green, and whether they chase subsidies. | `applyPolicies`, `companyStance` in `geo.ts` |
| **Cities** | About 160 labor markets: real metros plus one to four named regions per state. Each city has its own cost of living, tech and manufacturing depth, tax breaks, permit speed, local minimum wage, union strength, power price and grid capacity. Cities can pass data-center moratoriums when the grid fills up, and lift them later. The Build panel compares every city on hireable workers, wages, cost, build time, local opinion and power. | `src/sim/geo.ts`, `src/sim/data/cities.ts` |
| **Local opinion** | Every city holds an opinion of every company. Hiring, local investment and HQ amenities raise it; layoffs, closures and straining the grid lower it. Opinion changes who applies, how fast permits clear, whether you get incentives, and below 15% the city denies permits outright. | `opinionOf`, `permitMult` in `geo.ts` |
| **Jobs** | 24 occupations in four groups (blue collar, service, office and professional, tech and science), from farmworkers and oil and gas workers to nurses, lawyers and data scientists. Each has its own wage, political lean, ladder into better jobs, and geography (oil workers in oil states, farmworkers outside the metros). | `src/sim/data/jobs.ts` |
| **Labor market** | One labor pool per city × job. Companies compete for free workers each month, weighted by wage offer, how the job and the city feel about them, and the HQ. Hiring a big share of a pool, or running short of workers, pushes local wages up. | `updateLabor` in `engine.ts` |
| **Population** | Pops grow, move between states toward jobs and wages, drift between cities, and retrain up job ladders (for example, technicians to engineers) when demand is high. | `src/sim/population.ts` |
| **Headquarters** | A specific building in a specific city: a downtown tower, a suburban campus or a city block. Nine amenities (cafeteria, childcare, innovation lab, public plaza, commuter shuttles and more) each change hiring, research, admin capacity, reputation or local opinion, with upkeep that scales with HQ size. You can relocate. | `src/sim/hq.ts`, `src/ui/tabs/Headquarters.tsx` |
| **Buildings** | 24 types: HQ, R&D labs, assembly plants, chip fabs, design centers, battery and auto plants, software studios, data centers, analytics offices, ad platforms, food, apparel, consumer goods, media, pharma, power plants, oil and gas fields, refineries, steel mills, chemical plants, farms and solar panel factories. Each has levels (about 100 staff each) and production methods, such as conventional vs shale wells or row crops vs precision agriculture. Wells and farms only go where the resource is (`src/sim/data/resources.ts`); richer basins and farmland produce more, sunnier states get more from solar. | `src/sim/data/buildings.ts` |
| **Industries** | Every building belongs to an industry. Know-how starts low when you enter a new one (higher if you're in a related industry) and grows over about three years; until then output is lower and running costs up to 50% higher. Scale: efficiency rises from about 78% at one level to 100% at 150 levels. Each industry past your second adds 15% to HQ workload, and investors discount sprawling conglomerates by up to 25%. | `src/sim/sectors.ts` |
| **Goods & supply chains** | Parts → chips → AI accelerators → cloud → data platforms; batteries + chips + steel → cars; crude oil → gasoline and diesel; natural gas → electricity, steel and chemicals; chemicals → crops, drugs, solar panels and household goods; crops → groceries; and so on. Inputs come from your own stock first, then the cheapest US seller, then imports. | `src/sim/data/goods.ts`, `produce` in `engine.ts` |
| **Needs** | Pops split their income across eight needs (food, energy, healthcare, clothing, transport, communication, computing, entertainment). Basic needs take more of a small paycheck. Goods compete within a need (EVs vs gas cars). How well needs are met gives each pop a standard of living, which drives politics. | `src/sim/consumers.ts`, `src/sim/data/needs.ts` |
| **Markets** | Demand comes from pops (weighted by income) plus the rest of the world. Buyers split it by quality² × reputation × brand awareness × price. An unmodeled "everyone else" competes in every market. | `clearMarket` in `engine.ts` |
| **Politics** | Democratic and Republican parties (simplified platforms); elections every two years, decided by occupation-based leanings, living standards, presidential approval and donations. Nine laws (tax, minimum wage, tariffs, antitrust, subsidies, immigration, unions, privacy, climate) with real effects. One bill at a time moves through Congress; lobbying speeds it up or kills it. | `src/sim/politics.ts`, `src/sim/data/laws.ts` |
| **Congress** | The real 535 members seated on 1 January 2026 (from the open [congress-legislators](https://github.com/unitedstates/congress-legislators) dataset), each with a game faction: socialist, progressive, liberal, moderate or Blue Dog Democrat; moderate, corporate, MAGA, populist or libertarian Republican. Members vote their faction's positions; the Senate needs 60 votes except for tax and subsidy bills (reconciliation); presidents veto. Every House seat and a third of the Senate are contested each cycle, seat by seat. State legislatures shift with the national mood. Companies can register a PAC to back or oppose members, who then lean toward the company's lobbying when a vote is close. Regenerate with `scripts/build_congress.py`. | `src/sim/congress.ts`, `src/sim/data/congress.ts`, `src/sim/data/factions.ts` |
| **Macro** | Output gap with random recessions, inflation, a Fed that sets rates (which feed into borrowing costs and valuations), and world events like chip shortages, oil spikes and AI frenzies. | `src/sim/macro.ts` |
| **Research** | Seven tech tracks. Each level above the market baseline means about 8% better products. The baseline rises one level a year. | `research` in `engine.ts` |
| **Finance** | Daily cash flows, monthly books, quarterly earnings vs Wall Street expectations, dividends, a revolving credit line, debt covenants (six months over the limit means default), share issuance, buybacks. Market cap comes from earnings and sales multiples driven by growth and interest rates, times investor sentiment. | `closeMonth`, `earnings`, `fundamentalValue` |
| **Tiers** | Startup → Small → Mid → Large → Mega Cap → Titan. You're ranked against every company in your tier. | `src/sim/tiers.ts` |
| **Elections** | Each of the 435 House districts starts from its real 2024 presidential vote and the incumbent's 2024 overperformance. The 2026 Senate map includes real retirements and the Florida and Ohio specials. Primaries follow the state calendar and can unseat incumbents; races carry Safe, Likely, Lean or Toss-up ratings that move with the national environment. Presidential years use the Electoral College. | `src/sim/elections.ts`, `src/sim/data/districts.ts` |
| **M&A** | You make an offer at a premium you choose. The target's board wants more from a bigger buyer and from a company that's doing well. Big or overlapping deals draw a regulatory second request that can take months and block the deal, especially under strict antitrust law or a populist or progressive Congress. A blocked deal costs a 4% break fee. | `src/sim/deals.ts`, `offer` in `actions.ts` |
| **World** | Twelve countries, each a labor market of hireable pops with local wages, productivity, incentives and risk. Output sold into the US pays the country's tariff plus freight; intermediates are assumed to go half to the US. Offshoring lowers what American factory workers and towns think of you. About half a million immigrant workers a year (scaled by immigration law) join American pops, settling where their communities live. | `src/sim/intl.ts`, `src/sim/data/countries.ts` |
| **Leadership** | Five executives per company, each with a skill (1–5) and traits (visionary, cost cutter, dealmaker, celebrity, scandal-prone…) that change research, output, marketing, borrowing costs, valuation and more. Rivals poach your best people; you can hire theirs. The board's confidence tracks your stock against your tier; it votes on acquisitions, share issues, HQ moves and megaprojects, replaces directors at the annual meeting, and fires CEOs it has lost faith in. Activists buy stakes in laggards, make demands and run proxy fights. | `src/sim/leadership.ts` |
| **Roster** | Each game rolls its cast from a seed: most household-name companies plus dozens of generated ones sized against their market. | `src/sim/roster.ts` |
| **Rivals** | AI companies price to clear their stock, expand when they sell out, keep R&D and HQ in step with their size, borrow, buy back stock and make acquisitions. New startups appear over time. | `src/sim/ai.ts` |

The simulation in `src/sim` is plain TypeScript with no UI dependencies, and it is deterministic for a given seed. The UI in `src/ui` is React.

## Data

The state demographics in `src/sim/data/states.ts` are **approximate**, typed in by hand. To switch to real census data:

1. Download ACS PUMS person files from the [Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/microdata/access.html) or make an extract on [IPUMS USA](https://usa.ipums.org) with `ST, PWGTP, ESR, HISP, RAC1P, RAC2P, OCCP`.
2. `python3 scripts/pums_to_pops.py data/raw/psam_p*.csv`
3. Rebuild. The script writes `src/sim/data/popsOverride.ts`, and the game uses it automatically.

Check the race and occupation code lists in the script against the data dictionary for the survey year you download.

House district results for 2024 come from [jaytimm/PresElectionResults](https://github.com/jaytimm/PresElectionResults) and the [MIT Election Data and Science Lab](https://electionlab.mit.edu); regenerate with `scripts/build_districts.py`. The game uses the 2024 district lines and does not model the 2025–26 mid-decade redistricting. NY-21's figures are estimated. 2026 Senate candidates come from the declared contenders as of mid-2026 (`src/sim/data/contenders2026.ts`; the game still runs its own primaries). Other new candidates are drawn mostly from real politicians in the state: former members of Congress (from congress-legislators) and current state legislators and statewide officials (from [Open States](https://github.com/openstates/people)), regenerated with `scripts/build_politician_pool.py`. The rest are generated from the name pools (`scripts/build_names.py`). City figures are rough.

Starting company financials are rough mid-2020s figures for each company's real-world counterpart. All companies are fictional. Politicians are real people at game start, portrayed as satire; factions are the game's shorthand, assigned by hand from caucus membership and public positioning (see `scripts/build_congress.py`), and state legislature seat counts are approximate.
